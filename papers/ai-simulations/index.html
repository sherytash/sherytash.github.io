When and How to Integrate AI Simulations into Behavioral Experimentation

Herytash, S. · Solo-authored. Revise & Resubmit at International Journal of Research in Marketing (Academic Journal Guide 4).

Ask for the manuscript

Abstract

Purpose. Behavioral experiments are the gold standard for identifying causal effects of firm interventions, yet they are costly and slow to scale. This study asks when and how large language model (LLM) simulations can complement human experimentation, and validates the answer against a benchmark of 70 treatment effects from 30 peer-reviewed marketing studies.

Design/methodology/approach. I assemble a corpus of randomized, text-only contrasts from five leading marketing journals, simulate each experiment with an open-weights LLM, and compare predicted to human-observed effect sizes on prespecified metrics. Every statistic, table, and figure is generated from a single published effect-level dataset by one deterministic evaluation script. Boundary conditions are examined by cluster-robust meta-regression, and a hybrid human–AI design based on prediction-powered inference (PPI) is developed analytically.

Findings. Simulated and human effect sizes correlate substantially across studies (Pearson r = .73, 95% CI [.60, .83]), but predictions are systematically attenuated (calibration slope .38) and 67.1% fall within the human 95% confidence interval. The simulations rank effects well yet do not beat a corpus-grand-mean prior on absolute error, locating their value in discrimination, not calibration. Publication-bias diagnostics reveal strong small-study asymmetry in the underlying human corpus, so the benchmark measures fidelity to published effects. Estimation error shrinks with human sample size; preregistered design moderators show no reliable effects in this underpowered corpus.

Research implications. The results support a view of LLMs as population-level language priors: informative about the ordering of textual treatment effects, conservative about their magnitudes. Whether performance generalizes beyond the training distribution remains an open question that publication dates cannot settle.

Practical implications. For screening and design triage, LLM simulations provide near-zero-marginal-cost rankings. The PPI framework shows how biased simulations can supplement—never replace—human samples while preserving unbiasedness, with savings that grow in the (application-specific, here illustrative) individual-level human–LLM correlation.

Originality/value. The study contributes a fully auditable, open-weights, marketing-specific benchmark whose every number regenerates from public data with one script, and an honest account of what such simulations can and cannot yet deliver.
