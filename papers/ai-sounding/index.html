Sounding like AI Costs Ratings, Even for Strong Papers

Herytash, S. · Submitted to Research Policy (Financial Times 50; Academic Journal Guide 4*) as a Research Note.

Ask for the manuscript

Abstract

Journals and conferences now treat text that reads as written by large language models (LLMs) as a sign of weak work. We test this premise with all 50,243 submissions to the International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR) from 2021 to 2026 and their reviews. Before LLMs were available, papers that used the vocabulary typical of LLM text were rated like any other; from 2024 on, the same vocabulary costs ratings, with a sharp break on two measures available for every year and a penalty on a commercial detector in 2026. The penalty reaches papers that another reviewer rates highly, and most of it remains within the same authors. LLM-modified text is widespread at every rating level, in about 10% to 42% of abstracts from 2024 to 2026. On the same paper, confident reviewers mark AI-sounding text down more than their co-reviewers do. In decisions, the rating gap is worth about 12 percentage points of a fully AI-sounding paper's chance of acceptance. At equal ratings in 2021 to 2025 the committee adds no penalty, but authors withdraw such papers more often. Strong and weak work alike is now written with AI, and reviewers penalise its style even where other reviewers judge the work strong.
